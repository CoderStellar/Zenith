        Motivation

        Almost everyone has heard that the planet is changing, but almost no one can check what is happening where they actually live. The honest, qualified answers are locked inside research papers and data portals that assume you already know what a time series or a p-value is; the answers that do reach the public arrive as headlines with all of their uncertainty stripped out. We kept running into that gap ourselves, and wanted to close it: to let anyone ask a plain question about a real place and get back an answer that is both understandable and statistically honest.

        Working with isolated variables and disconnected datasets is genuinely hard, and that difficulty is the whole point of this challenge. The same underlying process can push a variable up in one region and down in another; the patterns you're looking for are often hidden by seasonal changes and natural year-to-year fluctuations; and there are hundreds of candidate variables to sort through before any analysis even begins. A tool that hid all of that and simply printed a confident arrow would be lying. That's why we built Zenith: to pick sensible NASA variables for a region, account for seasonal patterns, analyze long-term trends, and then actually test whether that trend is strong enough to believe, and say so plainly when it is not. Built using real mathematical models.

        What we kept coming back to is the idea of turning people into data detectives rather than passive readers of someone else's conclusion. “No clear trend yet” is an honest result, and sometimes all a trend needs to cross the line into significance is a few more years of data. Zenith is our attempt to make that kind of careful, evidence-first reasoning about a changing planet something a student, a teacher, or a curious neighbour can do in a few clicks, without having to take our word, or anyone's, on faith.

        Methodology

        1. Regions: US Census 2024 state boundaries. Each grid cell is weighted by the fraction of its area within the state and by cos(latitude) to account for differences in grid-cell area. Alaska's boundary is clipped at 180°, excluding the far-western Aleutians.
        2. Data: Monthly NASA products, via NASA Earthdata (Harmony subsetting and Giovanni's averaging service): air temperature (MERRA-2 M2TMNXSLV, 1980–2024), precipitation (GPM IMERG V07, 2001–2024), aerosol optical depth (MODIS Aqua MYD08_M3, summers 2003–2024), total water storage (GRACE/GRACE-FO JPL mascons, 2003–2024) and soil moisture (GLDAS Noah 2.1, 2003–2024).
        3. Trend: A straight line fitted together with the seasonal cycle on the real monthly calendar, so gaps stay gaps (such as GRACE's 2017–18 gap). For smoke, the trend of each summer's June–August average.
        4. Significance: Neighbouring months are not independent, so the test uses an effective number of months from the lag-1 autocorrelation, the correction IPCC AR6 uses (Santer et al. 2008). The 95% range and p-value come from that test.
        5. Verdict: “Clear rise/fall” needs p < 0.05 and a robust Theil–Sen slope (same correction) pointing the same way. Otherwise “no clear trend yet”: the change so far sits within normal year-to-year swings, so it may still be noise, which is not “no trend”. A longer record may settle it.
        6. Explanations: The “What this means” text is written by an AI model from a fact sheet of the computed numbers. A checker rejects any number not in the results, any causal claim (“caused by”, “proves”) and filler; if a reply fails twice, a plain template sentence is shown instead.
        7. Units: Temperature in °C per decade; precipitation and soil moisture as % of the state's average per decade; smoke in aerosol optical depth per decade; water storage in mm/year (and km³/yr for the whole state).
        8. Pipeline: scripts/build_tool_data.py (in the earthpull project) downloads, clips, averages and tests everything, then writes data.js. The site itself is static and does no processing.

        Correlation engine

        Available in the Try tab, the correlation engine explores which environmental variables move together across a selected region. No AI: fixed statistics and a fixed physics table. Identifies relationships and potential physical connections without claiming causation.

        1. Clean-up: Each calendar month's normal value is removed (seasons), then each variable's trend is removed with the same fit as the trend test, so two variables that merely both drift are not called linked.
        2. Move together? Lagged correlation (0–3 months, either direction), with an effective sample size from each series' lag-1 autocorrelation (Bretherton et al. 1999), a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery correction across all pairs and delays, and a surrogate test (phase-randomised series that keep each variable's own rhythm).
        3. Not explained by the rest? Partial correlation, controlling for every other variable chosen.
        4. Timing: A Granger causality test in both directions (does A's past help predict B?), used only to flag one-way timing.
        5. Physics: A fixed table of 26 known mechanisms (e.g. rain → soil moisture, warmer air → drier soil, snowfall → snow depth). Only listed pairs, with the expected sign and delay, reach the top verdict. "Same" pairs (two measures of one thing) should agree; "shared" pairs (a common source) are never called a cause.
        6. Is a trend explained by another variable? The variable is refitted with the other one added (same memory-adjusted errors). If the other has no significant effect of its own, or is itself just a steady trend with no known mechanism, the engine reports that the two "cannot be separated".

        Guardrails

        • Trends, not causes: The tool reports what changed and how sure the statistics are. It does not say why.
        • Not a forecast: Past trends do not predict the future, and nothing here is emergency, insurance, planning or legal advice.
        • Resolution limits: State averages hide large local differences. GRACE's footprint is about 300 km, so Hawaii's water-storage trend is not reliable, and state totals leak across borders (shown as a range).
        • Relative charts: The change profile ranks these five states against each other only.
        • Tested against published studies: All 25 checks against published studies were run; 24 matched the published result (96%), and the one miss is explained by the dataset. The dataset limitations they revealed are listed below.
        • Attribution: Built with NASA open data. Not affiliated with or endorsed by NASA.
        • Privacy and accessibility: No accounts, tracking or personal data. Keyboard-navigable tabs, labelled controls, and text versions (ranking list, detail table, profile list) of every chart.

          Future scope

          Zenith's honest limits double as its roadmap. The engine already runs for any point on Earth in “Try it out yourself”; the work ahead is to bring that same depth to the main site and let people go well past five demo states.

          • Finer regions: let people draw their own area, or pick a city or county, instead of whole-state averages that hide local change.
          • Global coverage: extend the prepared demo data worldwide, so anywhere a person cares about answers quickly, not just the Pacific states.
          • More variables and non-linear trends: add ocean, atmosphere and vegetation variables, and detect changepoints and curved trends, not only straight lines.
          • From correlation toward attribution: move carefully beyond “what moves together” toward tested statements about cause, with the physics table carrying more of the weight.
          • Watchlists: let people follow a place and a variable, and hear about it when a “no clear trend yet” crosses into significance as new data arrives.

          Run it

          The Homepage and Map tabs work as a static site. "Try it out yourself" needs the tool server, which fetches NASA data on demand (a free NASA Earthdata login is required on the server):

          .venv/bin/uvicorn earthpull.api:app --port 8000 then open http://localhost:8000

          Variables with a global file on the server (air temperature, aerosol, MODIS AOD, GRACE) answer in seconds anywhere on Earth; others take from seconds (Giovanni averaging) to a few minutes the first time, then are cached.
